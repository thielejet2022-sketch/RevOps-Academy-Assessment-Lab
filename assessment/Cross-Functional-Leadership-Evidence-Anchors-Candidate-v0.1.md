# Cross-Functional Leadership — Evidence Anchors

**Status:** Candidate v0.1  
**Date:** 2026-08-30  
**Canonical:** No  
**Parent framework:** RevOps Competency Model Candidate v0.4  
**Purpose:** Test whether the shared L1–L5 proficiency spine transfers cleanly to a behavioral and relational competency.

## Competency Definition
Ability to influence stakeholders, clarify accountability, manage competing incentives, facilitate decisions, and drive operating alignment without relying only on formal authority.

This competency evaluates what a participant **does to create alignment and action across organizational boundaries**, not whether they sound collaborative, are personally likable, hold a senior title, or can describe generic stakeholder-management principles.

---

# Construct Boundary

Cross-Functional Leadership is related to but distinct from:

- **Communication & Executive Readout:** how clearly and appropriately the participant frames and communicates the situation and decision;
- **Change Management & Adoption:** how an approved operating change is translated into sustained behavior and adoption;
- **Operating Cadence & Execution:** how recurring inspection, accountability, and follow-through are structured;
- **GTM Systems Thinking:** how well the participant understands the interconnected revenue system;
- **Commercial & Business Judgment:** the quality of the underlying business decision and tradeoff.

Cross-Functional Leadership asks:

> **Can this person cause people with different goals, incentives, authority, and information to reach and execute a useful operating decision?**

---

# L1 — Foundational

## Core signal
**Participates constructively in cross-functional work with guidance and recognizes basic stakeholder dependencies.**

## Observable positive evidence
- identifies obvious stakeholders affected by a defined task or decision;
- shares relevant information when prompted;
- distinguishes own responsibility from another team's responsibility;
- follows established escalation or decision paths;
- recognizes straightforward ownership gaps or handoff problems;
- can support a cross-functional meeting or action plan when structure is provided.

## Boundary test
**Can the participant contribute effectively once the stakeholders, decision path, and responsibilities have been defined for them?**

If yes, L1 may be appropriate.

## Does not earn L2 merely by
- attending many meetings;
- being agreeable;
- copying stakeholders on messages;
- saying “we need alignment”;
- escalating routine disagreements to a manager.

---

# L2 — Practitioner

## Core signal
**Independently coordinates familiar cross-functional work and resolves routine ownership or alignment issues.**

## Observable positive evidence
- identifies the relevant stakeholders for familiar work without prompting;
- clarifies routine ownership, inputs, dependencies, and deadlines;
- surfaces ordinary disagreements early rather than allowing silent drift;
- follows up with owners and closes routine coordination loops;
- adapts communication to common stakeholder needs;
- resolves familiar handoff or priority conflicts using established decision rights;
- escalates selectively when authority or risk exceeds the participant's scope.

## Boundary from L1
The transition is **guided participation → independent coordination of familiar cross-functional work**.

## Boundary test
**Can the participant independently create enough alignment for routine work to move without a manager structuring every interaction?**

## Does not earn L3 merely by
- running meetings efficiently;
- creating a RACI;
- sending detailed follow-ups;
- maintaining strong personal relationships;
- coordinating a project whose goals and incentives are already aligned.

---

# L3 — Advanced

## Core signal
**Builds alignment and drives decisions through ambiguous cross-functional problems where stakeholders have materially different perspectives, incentives, or constraints.**

## Observable positive evidence
- identifies the real decision and the stakeholders who can make, block, influence, or execute it;
- surfaces conflicting incentives rather than treating disagreement as a communication failure;
- distinguishes factual disagreement from incentive, priority, authority, or risk disagreement;
- creates a decision process appropriate to the ambiguity and stakes;
- translates competing functional concerns into shared operating terms;
- challenges stakeholders without unnecessarily escalating conflict;
- uses evidence to narrow disagreement while recognizing where judgment is still required;
- clarifies ownership after a decision and ensures affected functions understand their responsibilities;
- can make progress without possessing formal authority over all participants;
- recognizes when consensus is unnecessary and a clear decision owner should decide.

## Boundary from L2
L2 coordinates familiar work. L3 **creates alignment when the work itself is ambiguous and stakeholder interests are not naturally aligned**.

## Boundary test
**Did the participant coordinate people around a known answer, or help the organization reach a defensible answer despite real cross-functional tension?**

## Does not earn L4 merely by
- facilitating a difficult meeting well;
- gaining executive sponsorship;
- resolving one high-profile conflict;
- having broad organizational relationships;
- persuading everyone to agree with the participant's preferred solution.

---

# L4 — Strategic

## Core signal
**Shapes consequential cross-functional decisions and the governance, decision rights, incentives, or accountability mechanisms that determine how functions work together.**

## Observable positive evidence
- frames material decisions so executives/functions can make explicit tradeoffs rather than defend local metrics;
- identifies when recurring conflict is caused by operating-model design, incentives, decision rights, or ownership rather than personalities;
- redesigns decision rights or governance where ambiguity repeatedly creates friction;
- aligns functional goals or success measures around end-to-end revenue outcomes when appropriate;
- influences senior leaders to change local priorities for the health of the revenue system;
- makes productive disagreement visible and prevents false consensus;
- establishes mechanisms for resolving future cross-functional conflicts without requiring personal intervention every time;
- anticipates how a decision will alter incentives, behavior, power, workload, or accountability across functions;
- protects legitimate functional differences instead of forcing alignment where variation is useful;
- creates clear executive accountability for decisions that otherwise fall between functions.

## Boundary from L3
L3 resolves ambiguous cross-functional problems. L4 **changes how consequential cross-functional decisions, incentives, ownership, or conflicts are governed**.

## Boundary test
**Did the participant successfully lead a difficult cross-functional decision, or did they also improve the operating conditions under which future cross-functional decisions will be made?**

## Does not earn L4 merely by
- interacting with executives;
- chairing a steering committee;
- creating more governance meetings;
- obtaining consensus;
- using “alignment,” “accountability,” or “shared goals” language;
- having formal authority over several functions.

---

# L5 — Enterprise / Architect

## Core signal
**Creates reusable leadership and governance architecture that enables effective cross-functional decisions across materially different organizational contexts without requiring uniform structures or personal intervention.**

## Observable positive evidence
- distinguishes durable collaboration/decision principles from context-specific stakeholder structures;
- designs decision-rights and accountability frameworks that remain useful across different GTM motions, business units, products, geographies, acquisitions, or portfolio companies;
- establishes principles for when decisions should be centralized, delegated, federated, or escalated;
- governs justified variation in functional ownership while preserving clear enterprise outcomes;
- designs mechanisms that remain effective as leaders, organizational structures, incentives, and market conditions change;
- anticipates recurring classes of cross-functional conflict and creates reusable resolution logic;
- creates leadership systems other operators can use without depending on the original architect's relationships or personal influence;
- balances enterprise coherence with local autonomy rather than forcing identical organizational models;
- recognizes when the leadership architecture itself needs to evolve as complexity changes.

## Boundary from L4
L4 shapes strategic cross-functional governance within a complex operating system. L5 **creates transferable governance and decision architecture for materially different operating contexts and their evolution**.

## Boundary test
**Did the participant improve how this organization leads across functions, or demonstrate a reusable architecture for preserving effective cross-functional leadership across different organizations or materially different operating contexts?**

## Critical anti-bias rule
L5 does not require a Fortune 500 employer, multiple portfolio companies, a C-suite title, or formal authority across functions. Those environments may create opportunities to demonstrate the construct, but scale and title are not the construct.

# Cross-Level Summary

| Level | Cross-Functional Leadership signal |
|---|---|
| **L1 Foundational** | Participates constructively with guidance and recognizes stakeholder dependencies |
| **L2 Practitioner** | Independently coordinates familiar cross-functional work |
| **L3 Advanced** | Creates alignment and decisions amid ambiguity and competing incentives |
| **L4 Strategic** | Shapes consequential decisions plus governance, incentives, decision rights, and accountability |
| **L5 Enterprise / Architect** | Creates reusable cross-functional governance architecture across materially different contexts |

# Adversarial Calibration Cases

## Case A — The Beloved Collaborator Trap
A participant is universally described as easy to work with, responsive, positive, and a strong meeting facilitator. They avoid direct disagreement, seek consensus on nearly every decision, and escalate when functional leaders remain opposed.

**Expected interpretation:** Positive interpersonal behavior is not sufficient evidence of advanced Cross-Functional Leadership. Depending on independent coordination evidence, this may be L1–L2.

## Case B — The Forceful Executive Trap
A senior leader resolves cross-functional conflict quickly by making the decision personally and directing teams to comply. Decisions move fast, but functions do not understand the tradeoffs, recurring conflicts return, and execution depends on the leader's authority.

**Expected interpretation:** Decisiveness and hierarchy are not automatically L4. The participant may demonstrate strong decision authority while showing limited evidence of cross-functional leadership architecture.

## Case C — The Meeting/Governance Inflation Trap
A participant creates a weekly steering committee, monthly executive review, RACI, Slack channel, escalation process, and dashboard whenever teams are misaligned.

**Expected interpretation:** More coordination infrastructure is not higher proficiency. The evaluator should ask whether the mechanism addresses the actual source of conflict and improves decisions or simply adds organizational drag.

## Case D — The Consensus Trap
A participant refuses to proceed until Marketing, Sales, Finance, and CS all agree on the solution.

**Expected interpretation:** Consensus is not the goal. Advanced leadership includes knowing when to seek alignment, when to expose disagreement, and when a legitimate decision owner should decide.

## Case E — The Relationship-Dependent Architect Trap
A VP can personally get any functional leader to cooperate because of trust accumulated over years. When the VP is absent, decisions stall and old conflicts return.

**Expected interpretation:** This may demonstrate significant L3/L4 personal leadership but is evidence against L5 if the operating system depends on the individual's relationships rather than durable governance.

# Assessment Design Implications

## Behavioral evidence cannot rely only on stated intent
A participant saying “I would align stakeholders” is weak evidence. Better assessments should reveal:
- who disagrees and why;
- conflicting incentives or success metrics;
- incomplete authority;
- stakeholder reactions to the participant's choices;
- consequences of escalation, consensus-seeking, or unilateral action;
- whether the participant changes approach when resistance reveals new information.

## Dynamic stakeholder simulation may be necessary
Cross-Functional Leadership may require interactive scenarios where stakeholder positions respond to participant behavior. A static written answer may test leadership reasoning but provide weaker evidence of actual leadership behavior.

## Work-history evidence may complement scenarios
Because influence and trust develop over time, credible examples from prior work may provide useful evidence when structured around observable behavior, tradeoffs, resistance, outcomes, and what persisted after the participant's intervention.

Do not treat unverified success claims as equivalent to observed assessment behavior.

# Construct Separation Pressure Test

The shared proficiency spine transfers to Cross-Functional Leadership, but this test exposes a larger risk than the previous two vertical slices: **competency contamination**.

A participant could make a brilliant commercial decision but lead stakeholders poorly. Another could create excellent alignment around a commercially weak decision. These should not collapse into one score.

Similarly:
- clear executive communication does not prove leadership;
- successful adoption does not by itself prove the original cross-functional decision process was strong;
- a good operating cadence does not prove conflicts are being resolved productively;
- formal authority does not prove influence without authority.

This supports keeping Cross-Functional Leadership distinct while requiring evaluators to cite the specific behavioral evidence used for the score.

---

# Transfer-Test Result

The five-level spine transfers conceptually to a behavioral/relational competency:

1. guided participation;
2. independent familiar coordination;
3. ambiguous alignment and decision leadership;
4. strategic governance of cross-functional decisions/incentives/accountability;
5. reusable leadership/governance architecture across materially different contexts.

However, this vertical slice reveals that **assessment modality matters by competency**. Analytical and technical competencies can often be tested substantially through evidence-rich cases. Cross-Functional Leadership likely requires dynamic stakeholder interaction and/or structured work-history evidence for stronger confidence.

That is not a failure of the shared proficiency spine. It is evidence that a common competency model may require **different evidence-collection methods** by competency.

---

# Implications for the Parent Model

This third materially different vertical slice increases confidence in:
- one shared L1–L5 proficiency spine;
- competency-specific evidence anchors;
- separation of proficiency from Confidence and Role Fit;
- maintaining Cross-Functional Leadership as a distinct competency;
- using competency-appropriate assessment modalities rather than forcing every competency into the same exercise format.

It also creates a new design question:

> **Should the competency model explicitly identify preferred evidence modalities for each competency?**

Possible modalities may include:
- analytical/dynamic case;
- technical/system design case;
- stakeholder simulation;
- structured behavioral/work-history evidence;
- artifact/work-product review;
- repeated longitudinal observation.

Do not add this architecture automatically. It should be pressure-tested before incorporation into the parent model.

## Recommended Next Step
Pressure-test the newly exposed **evidence modality** question before creating more competency anchors. Determine whether modality belongs in the competency model itself, the assessment methodology layer, or only individual exercise design.
