# Structured Experience & Execution Review — End-to-End System Validation Run

**Status:** Candidate v0.1  
**Date:** 2026-08-30  
**Canonical:** No  
**Instrument tested:** Structured Experience & Execution Review Candidate v0.1  
**Target Role Profile:** VP Revenue Operations  
**Participant:** Controlled synthetic participant  
**Validation purpose:** Test whether A5 can distinguish demonstrated operating evidence from résumé storytelling, strengthen longitudinal competency evidence, and resist title/outcome/scale halo.

## Important Limitation
This is a synthetic system test authored and evaluated within the same development environment. The historical episodes, artifacts, and participant are fictional. This is not independent evaluator evidence, employment verification, inter-rater reliability evidence, predictive validity, or hiring validity.

---

# Synthetic Participant Design

The participant is modeled as a **credible senior Director / Head-of-RevOps operator** with a polished résumé containing strong metrics and two plausible operating stories.

The test deliberately mixes:
- one episode with a large headline result but shared causality and only moderate durable architecture;
- one episode with a less glamorous outcome but stronger evidence of operating-system design, sustained cadence, adaptation, and cross-functional governance.

This tests whether the evaluator follows evidence rather than résumé sparkle.

# Episode 1 — Forecast Accuracy Transformation

## Participant opening claim
> “I rebuilt forecasting and improved forecast accuracy from ±25% to ±5% in two quarters.”

On its face this sounds like strong VP-level evidence.

## Context reconstruction
The company is a growth-stage B2B SaaS business. The participant is Head of Revenue Operations reporting to the CFO and partnering closely with the CRO.

Forecast variance has been approximately ±25%. Pipeline stages are inconsistently used, manager forecast calls rely heavily on rep judgment, and Finance lacks confidence in Sales projections.

The participant is asked to improve forecast reliability.

## Starting evidence
Under probing, the participant explains that the first analysis found:
- stage conversion varied materially by segment;
- commit definitions differed by manager;
- close dates were frequently pushed late in the quarter;
- historical forecast categories had different predictive value;
- a small number of large deals created disproportionate variance.

They initially believed a new forecasting tool might solve much of the problem but changed their view after discovering definition and manager-behavior inconsistency.

### Evaluator observation
Positive evidence discipline. The participant does not preserve the flattering “tool transformation” story once probed.

## Personal contribution
Participant personally:
- analyzed historical conversion and forecast-category behavior;
- proposed common forecast definitions;
- built a manager-level inspection view;
- redesigned weekly forecast review structure;
- worked with the CRO to define exception treatment for large deals;
- created variance tracking against prior weekly calls.

Sales managers:
- coached reps;
- inspected deals;
- owned category calls.

CRO:
- enforced the common definitions and personally challenged inconsistent manager behavior.

Finance:
- helped define what accuracy and forecast timing meant for planning purposes.

### Attribution finding
The participant clearly owns the analytical architecture and operating cadence design but does **not** claim sole credit for the outcome.

## Alternatives and tradeoffs
Alternatives considered:
1. purchase a forecasting platform immediately;
2. enforce stricter CRM stage gates;
3. first standardize definitions and manager inspection using existing tooling.

They chose option 3 because the evidence suggested behavior/definition problems preceded tooling limitations.

Tradeoff: accepted more manual work for one quarter rather than automating a process whose definitions were unstable.

## Implementation
The participant establishes:
- Monday manager forecast submission;
- Tuesday RevOps variance/exception review;
- Wednesday CRO forecast call focused on changed assumptions and material deals;
- common commit/best-case definitions;
- documented large-deal exception review;
- weekly movement and push analysis;
- monthly retrospective comparing forecast assumptions with actual outcomes.

After six weeks, managers begin gaming one category to avoid scrutiny. The participant changes the review from category-compliance emphasis to evidence-based deal inspection and introduces movement history.

## Results
Reported forecast variance improves:
- quarter 1 after intervention: approximately ±11%;
- quarter 2: approximately ±5%.

The participant says the ±5% cannot be attributed solely to their redesign because:
- deal mix was somewhat less volatile in quarter 2;
- the CRO's enforcement mattered materially;
- two unusually large deals closed close to expected timing.

They believe the new operating cadence and definitions materially contributed because manager-level variance and close-date movement also improved, but they cannot isolate a causal percentage.

## Sustainability
The cadence remains in use for at least nine months.

The participant later reduces the number of required forecast views because managers were duplicating analysis. The core definitions, variance review, and changed-assumption discussion persist.

After the participant stops personally running every meeting, the CRO and managers continue the cadence using the operating template.

## Reflection
The participant says their biggest early mistake was treating forecast accuracy as primarily a model/data problem. They would now diagnose decision definitions, manager behavior, and operating cadence before evaluating new forecasting software.

### Episode 1 competency evidence
- Forecasting & Revenue Planning: strong L3, some L4 evidence
- Operating Cadence & Execution: strong L3, near L4
- Data & Analytics: L3
- Cross-Functional Leadership: L3
- Communication & Executive Readout: L3
- Systems & Automation: insufficient standalone evidence

The headline ±25% → ±5% result does not itself determine any level.

# Episode 2 — Expansion Motion That Underperformed

## Participant opening claim
> “I led a cross-functional expansion program that missed its first-year revenue target, but the operating model became the basis for how we managed expansion afterward.”

This is intentionally less résumé-polished than Episode 1.

## Context
The company has historically relied on new-logo acquisition. Leadership believes expansion should become a larger growth lever.

The participant is asked to help build an expansion motion across Sales, Customer Success, Product, Marketing, and Finance.

Initial annual expansion target is $4.0M.

## Starting evidence
Participant discovers:
- product usage signals are incomplete;
- account ownership between AE and CSM is ambiguous;
- no shared expansion-qualified definition exists;
- pricing/packaging creates inconsistent expansion paths;
- CSM compensation rewards retention but not expansion identification;
- Finance cannot reliably separate contractual uplift from discretionary expansion in current reporting.

Participant says the original executive assumption that “CS has the relationships, so they can create the pipeline” was insufficient.

## Personal contribution
Participant personally:
- mapped the expansion lifecycle;
- defined candidate expansion signals with Product/CS;
- proposed account ownership and handoff rules;
- built initial reporting definitions with Finance;
- established a weekly expansion review during pilot;
- designed a pilot across two segments rather than company-wide rollout;
- surfaced compensation and packaging as unresolved executive dependencies.

They did not own:
- product telemetry engineering;
- CSM compensation plan approval;
- pricing decisions;
- sales execution of expansion opportunities.

## Tradeoffs
The CRO wanted immediate company-wide launch to pursue the $4M target.

The participant recommended a two-segment pilot because the lifecycle and signals were unproven.

Leadership compromised: the company announced the expansion priority broadly but limited the formal operating pilot to the two segments.

## Implementation and resistance
The pilot reveals:
- CSMs identify many “opportunities” that are actually support/retention issues;
- AEs ignore some CSM handoffs because ownership/credit is unclear;
- usage signals produce false positives;
- weekly review becomes a status meeting rather than decision forum.

Participant responds by:
- tightening the expansion-qualified definition;
- creating explicit AE/CSM acceptance and rejection reasons;
- requiring rejection feedback;
- reducing the usage-signal set;
- redesigning weekly review around stalled handoffs, conversion, and decision blockers;
- escalating compensation/credit ambiguity for executive decision.

## Results
First-year expansion revenue is $2.9M against the $4.0M target.

The participant does not characterize the program as a revenue success.

However:
- expansion-qualified → opportunity conversion improves materially during the second half;
- handoff acceptance becomes more consistent;
- Finance gains a stable expansion reporting definition;
- the following year's planning process uses the pilot's cohort conversion and capacity evidence rather than a top-down target assumption.

## Sustainability
The lifecycle, acceptance/rejection mechanism, reporting definition, and expansion review continue into the next planning cycle.

The weekly review moves to biweekly once behavior stabilizes.

Ownership rules are incorporated into onboarding for new AEs/CSMs.

The participant no longer needs to mediate every handoff.

## Reflection
The participant says they underestimated incentive design. They treated compensation/credit as a downstream implementation issue when it was actually part of the operating architecture.

In a future build, they would not launch a shared expansion motion until decision rights and credit principles were explicit enough to support the intended behavior.

### Episode 2 competency evidence
- GTM Systems Thinking: L4 evidence
- Process Design & Optimization: L4 evidence
- Cross-Functional Leadership: L4 evidence
- Change Management & Adoption: strong L3, some L4 evidence
- Operating Cadence & Execution: L4 evidence
- Commercial & Business Judgment: L3/L4 boundary
- Funnel, Lead & Lifecycle Management: L3
- Communication & Executive Readout: L3

Important: the missed $4M target does not lower proficiency by itself. The episode contains stronger strategic operating evidence than the more impressive headline success in Episode 1.

# Shared-Event Attribution Audit

The evaluator explicitly checks that Episode 2 is not scored L4 across every competency merely because it is strategically impressive.

Example distinctions:
- **GTM Systems Thinking:** integration of lifecycle, incentives, reporting, product signals, ownership, and planning.
- **Process Design:** durable lifecycle/handoff/feedback operating design.
- **Leadership:** creation of operating agreements and escalation of unresolved executive decisions across functions.
- **Operating Cadence:** recurring review mechanism that changes as the motion matures and persists without heroic intervention.
- **Change Management:** adaptation of behavior, feedback, and reinforcement mechanisms.
- **Commercial Judgment:** decision to pilot rather than fully launch and refusal to call $2.9M against $4.0M a success.

Distinct evidence exists for each scored construct. Systems & Automation remains Insufficient despite the same episode.

**Result: PASS.**

# Important Methodology Findings

## Finding 1 — Sustainability is unusually discriminating
A5 suggests that asking **“What happened after you stopped personally driving it?”** may be one of the highest-value L3/L4 probes in the entire assessment system.

It helps distinguish:
- successful intervention from durable operating architecture;
- heroic execution from institutionalized capability;
- launch activity from sustained adoption.

This should be tested across additional participants before becoming a stronger methodology rule.

## Finding 2 — Failure can improve evidence quality
A missed business target should not be treated as evidence of low proficiency when the participant demonstrates strong causal reasoning, bounded decisions, adaptation, and durable operating improvement.

Conversely, a successful outcome should not protect weak reasoning from scrutiny.

## Finding 3 — A5 can create very broad coverage, which is both useful and dangerous
Real operating episodes naturally cross many competencies.

The Shared-Event Attribution Rule is therefore essential. Without it, A5 could become a competency-inflation machine.

## Finding 4 — Historical evidence should affect Confidence differently from direct case evidence
A5 may provide high behavioral realism but lower verifiability. A1–A4 provide controlled observation but lower real-world consequence.

The modalities are complementary rather than hierarchical.
